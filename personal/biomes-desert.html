Year

Biomes is an ongoing study of environments, tools and systems inside Unreal Engine 5.8. Each world starts from a simple idea I had back in college, and I build it out now with a production eye. Desert is the first: ten seconds in which a rock golem ambushes a mecha squad, eats a missile to the head and goes down.

The goal was a shot that reads like a finished game cinematic. The action is staged, the effects are timed to the animation, and everything renders cleanly through Movie Render Queue. With the models coming from Tripo3D, most of my time went into the production side: a rig and animation that cut together, effects tuned and timed to the frame, golden-hour light and fog, and a render that matches what I saw in the editor.

Vertical cut

The same beat, re-shot for phones as a "passing car" view. The camera rides a rail along a road I laid through the scene, and I hand-animated the aim so it tracks the golem. Two layered camera shakes (engine vibration and road bumps) plus five keyed jolts give it a handheld, in-the-car feel. Motion blur and a slow zoom finish it. One scene, two deliveries: 2.39:1 for the cinematic and 9:16 for social.

What I built

Modelled the cast and set pieces from my concepts with Tripo3D (image-to-3D), then set them up in Unreal.
Rigged the mecha with Unreal's modular Control Rig and hand-keyed its walk, plant and fire in Unreal.
Built the golem's performance from two Mixamo clips and fixed the leg pop at the seam on the FK rig.
Staged the full beat in Sequencer: one missile from the mecha's right-hand socket to the golem's head, then the hit and the fall.
Shaped the Niagara effects for this scene, tuned to read well on camera and stay light on performance, and timed each one to its frame: launch flash, missile trail, impact explosion and debris, fall dust, 10 footstep puffs, rifle muzzles, 38 bullet hits on the golem, truck smoke.
Glowing red golem eyes: a custom Niagara system attached to head sockets. Blue back-vent exhaust on the mecha.
Golden-hour lighting: a low warm sun (about 4200K) for long shadows, warm sky fill, volumetric fog for depth, and a warm grade with manual exposure and bloom.
Sound design with ElevenLabs sound effects, timed to the cut.
The "passing car" camera rig for the vertical cut: rail, shakes, jolts.
Render setup: one sequence per format and shared MRQ presets, rendered headless with Darkroom.

Toolkit

Tech notes

Effects firing at frame 0 in the final render. The editor looked right, but in Movie Render Queue the explosion and fall dust were already in the air at frame 0. With temporal sub-sampling, MRQ stretches spawn sections back to the shot start, so the effect spawns and bursts at frame 0. The fix was to key each effect's spawn on and off at its real frame instead of relying on where the section starts, applied to all 55 timed effects.
Muzzle flashes that fire only when the trigger is pulled. Keying a Niagara system's Auto Activate does nothing because it's read once at spawn. Each flash became a spawnable with a short section on the fire frame: it spawns, bursts and despawns.
Camera shakes built as assets, not keyframes. The car feel is two Perlin shake assets layered in Sequencer, so the speed and intensity can be tuned without re-keying the camera.

The journey

Desert is where the pipeline got built. It set up the render side the later worlds reuse: one sequence per format, shared Movie Render Queue presets, and lighting and wireframe passes rendered headless. It also set the effects workflow. Every effect is keyed on and off at its real frame, a lesson from the frame-0 bug, and one-shot effects like muzzle flashes are short spawnable sections. The render problems on this world, including a Desert job that got queued on the Forest map, are what led me to build Darkroom.

What's next

Sand simulation. Next I plan to make the sand react: footprints, displaced sand and trails behind the golem and the mecha, using Niagara fluids or a sand sim in place of the timed dust puffs.
Character simulation. I want to try physics-driven secondary motion and hit reactions, such as physics-based impacts from the bullet hits and a ragdoll blend on the golem's fall.
Creature study. The golem is worth coming back to as a deeper creature study, with its anatomy, rig and animation built for it from the ground up.
